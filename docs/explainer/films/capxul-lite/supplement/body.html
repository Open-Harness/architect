Capxul Lite, the whole design

A company's treasury on chain, in a contract of its own, controlled by its owners together. Budgets that people and agents can spend alone. Books that record every payment with its reason.

Two films say this aloud. The first describes the system. The second shows what falls out of the description and how it runs. This page has every file they quote, in the same order.

It is a design. Nothing here is compiled or run. Most of its syntax already exists in the repository or is decided. A line with an amber edge is proposed by this design.

The design rests on two layers.

Things last, and operations are short. A thing that waits says what it needs, and the framework asks.

When the other half of an act is only data, seal it. Then there is one operation, one signature, and nothing to wait for.

A chain store is a file. The contract takes its name from the file's path. The treasury is the root of that contract, so a factory makes one contract for each company, and every company has its own address. Seats, budgets and proposals are rows inside their own treasury's contract.

The factory changes an older decision. K25 says one deployment per network, switched on by each company's Safe. Capxul Lite has no Safe, so the treasury is the contract. Aaron decided the factory on 26 November 2026. It is not in the decision records yet.

Rules, events and refusals

A budget is active, exhausted or revoked. Spending has two outcomes. Renewing is a move made by time, in K23's words. Who may act is a role, and a role is data, because it has to mean the same thing in TypeScript and in Solidity.

The same goes for a rule. "Spent never passes the limit" is said once, as data, and both sides enforce it. Today only a unique rule across records exists. A rule inside one record would sit in a handler.

Every move records one event, in the same commit as the change. Events are the only thing that crosses from one place to another. Every refusal is declared once and gets one code, kept in a lock file that only ever grows. So "budget exceeded" is the same name from the contract, the command line and the app.

One act, sealed

Ada means one thing: pay this supplier, from my budget, for this reason. Two places have to change. The chain moves the money, and the reason can't go on a public chain. Her signed call already goes to our server, because the relayer is ours.

So the reason is sealed. There is one operation, on the chain, with one word on one field. The reason travels with her signed call to our server. The chain carries only its seal, a salted hash nobody can read back. She signs once, and that signature covers the money and the reason together.

Time runs down. The left column is the chain call's own state, which the framework keeps for every chain call.

On our side the spend is a record in the database, because an accountant needs lists. Nobody calls its first operation. The indexer hands on the event once the chain will keep it, and the record is written with its reason if we hold one.

Anyone can pay around the app, straight from a wallet. Then there is a seal with no reason behind it, and the spend is unexplained. An explanation has to match what was signed.

What sealing costs

  • Our server holds the contents. Losing them loses the reasons, though never the money.
  • It only fits when the other half is data. It can't help when someone else has to decide.
  • It touches the compiler, the contract generator, the relayer and the indexer.
  • It turns a K25 refusal, a private field on a public chain store, into an option.

A thing that waits

Not everything can be one act. A new budget needs two of the three owners, and they won't all be there at once. So something has to wait.

Operations are short. If something waits, it is a thing.

A grant is a proposal. It is open, then passed or expired. A waiting thing says what it needs, and the framework does the asking: each owner sees it waiting on them. If nobody answers, a clock closes it.

When the second owner approves, the proposal passes, and the budget is granted in the same transaction. Nobody calls the grant.

What a thing needs is already in its machine. From any waiting state, the moves out of it are the needs: who, what, and by when.

Because every way out is declared, a check can find a thing that could wait for ever. Here it finds something milder.

Film two · what falls out, and how it runs

What falls out

Every piece here is made from the description above, and stays in step with it. The blocks below are sketches of what would be generated.

The contract

The frame is generated. For each operation it checks the row is there, then the role, then the machine. It runs your handler, checks the rules, and emits the event. That is the same order the server uses. The only Solidity you write is behaviour. The reason never appears: the frame takes its seal and puts it in the event.

The server, and two actors

A route for each operation it serves, the same enforcer, and the database's tables. The relayer takes signed calls, keeps each reason under its seal, and pays the gas. The indexer reads the chain back. It knows every event from the blueprint, so nobody hands it an interface. It also keeps the list of budgets, because a contract has no list.

The name. The repository still says "follower" for the indexer. Aaron decided "indexer" on 26 November 2026, and the rename is filed for the framework goal.

Three ways in

The same spend, three times. You write none of them. An accountant, who never touches the chain, gets none of them.

The app

Each thing gives the app a reference: its state, the fields this caller may see, and the moves open to them right now. That is the hook and the headless components. A piece per operation, which K29 decided, stays beside it.

The infrastructure

Generated too: the server, its database, the two actors, and the contract's factory.

Proof

A journey runs in a world: a local chain, a local server, and a cast of people with wallets. A different agent writes it before the builder starts, and the builder never edits it.

A rule changes

Budgets renew every week, not every month. You change one line, and the model says what that touched.

This contract is live, with money in it. So the change is published as a new version, and nothing live moves. New companies get it. For the others, their owners take it up.

  • The compiler compares how each version stores its data. If an upgrade would corrupt it, the change has to be a move.
  • The portal shows which company is on which version.
  • Everything the change did not touch is still proven.

What is decided nowhere yet

  • Who deploys the contract, and where its address is kept. A checked-in record that only grows, like the refusals lock, would fit.
  • Production as an environment. A server can be promoted. A contract can only be deployed again from the same revision, and checked.
  • The order of bringing it up. The factory, then the indexer from its first block, then a funded relayer, then the server.
  • Taking it down. The server and database are destroyed. A staging contract stays on its test network for good.
  • The relayer's gas. Who funds it, and who is told when it runs low.

    What is still unsure, or open

    1. The link from a person to an account is in no goal.Paying a sealed spend does not need it. Reading the reason back in the app does, and so does every role that crosses.
    2. A role written as code can't be listed.Its holders can be offered a move. They can't be asked.
    3. Telling people is a new part.A "needs you" list falls out of the machines. How it reaches a person or an agent is not designed.
    4. "Once accepted, it finishes."An operation should promise that. Today the caller has to retry with its request key. How a database thing keeps the promise is not worked out.
    5. You model more things.Every intent that waits becomes a thing. A proposal is one. That is the price of keeping the order in the blueprint.
    6. Our server holds the sealed contents.They need the same care as the books: backed up, and never changed.
    7. A refusal on chain leaves no event.The relayer knows when a call is definitely refused. That knowledge has to be able to make a move.
    8. On a chain, what you can do is a guess.Only the contract knows, and things change between asking and signing. Trying the call first is the real answer.
    9. The contract's side of deployment.The five items under "Where it runs".
    10. A handler in two languages is two handlers.The frame keeps the order and the refusals the same. Only journeys show that the behaviour agrees.
