One button, two places

You didn't like the next step. So this section starts again from the facts, gives three solutions, and grades them.

The problem

Ada presses one button: Spend. She means one thing: pay this supplier 400 from my budget, for this reason. Underneath, two places have to change. Our server has to hold the reason. The chain has to move the money. Her wallet has to say yes.

Everything Orthograph makes, it makes per operation. If the blueprint says "two operations", every client has to know the recipe that joins them, and that recipe gets written by hand in the app, in a script and in an agent's prompt.

The facts nothing can change

  • Ada has one intent.
  • Money moves only when the chain says so, on her account's signature.
  • The reason must not be readable on a public chain.
  • Two places can never commit together. There is always a moment in between.
  • The chain can't see our server. Our server can see the chain.
  • The one I missed: her signed call already goes to our server, because the relayer is ours.

So the real question is where the join lives. It can live in the data, in a thing's states, or in the operation. Those are the three solutions.

1 · the join lives in the data

Seal it

There is one operation, on the chain. Its private fields travel with the signed call to our server. The chain gets only a seal of them: a salted hash. Ada signs once, and that one signature covers the money and the reason.

The draft, the "drafted" and "sent" states, and three operations all disappear. The waiting belongs to the framework, because every chain call already has tried, submitted and confirmed.

2 · the join lives in the thing's states

Offer the move

The spend is a thing of its own, and it is the intent. One of its moves is made by an operation in another place. A drafted spend offers that move to whoever drafted it. This is the next step, turned round so it hangs off the thing, not off an operation.

You still write the spend, its six moves and five operations. The client still makes two calls behind one button.

3 · the join lives in the operation

Ask for a yes

There is one operation, on our server. It cannot finish until someone says yes. A wallet's signature on a chain call is one kind of yes. An owner's approval is another. So is a person approving what an agent wants to do.

The framework holds the operation open, and closes it on yes, no or silence. That makes an operation long-lived, which nothing in the core is today.

Should the thing be the durable part?

What I think you mean, made as strong as I can

  • The unit is the thing, not the operation. A thing has an identity, its own state, and it handles one message at a time. That is an actor, in the Effect sense, wherever it happens to be stored.
  • An operation is a message to a thing. It should be short. If something has to wait, the thing waits, because the thing is what lasts.
  • A thing knows what it needs. In a waiting state it can say: I need a signature from my holder, or two more owners, or an event from the budget, or a date. It also knows what it doesn't need, so nothing else can move it.
  • The framework meets the need. It asks the right person, listens for the right event, sets the right timer. Nobody writes that by hand.
  • Things compose through needs. One thing's need is met by another thing's move. That is the whole of composition. No recipe between operations.
  • A contract is already this. It has an address, state, and takes one call at a time. So the chain and the server would be one model, and where a thing lives is a detail.

Is that right?

Yes, with one line drawn through the word durable. It means two things, and they belong in different places.

So I was wrong to hang "ask for a yes" on an operation. Hung on the thing, my main objection goes away. Operations stay one call and one commit. The answer to an ask is just another ordinary operation, checked like any other at the moment it arrives.

That also means solutions two and three were the same idea seen from two sides. A thing that is waiting on a person is both.

What it means for the framework

  • One rule the compiler can hold you to: if something has to wait, it is a thing.
  • One shape for every call: accepted, then done or refused. A chain call already works like that, and the relayer is the chain thing's inbox. Server things get the same promise.
  • A "needs you" list falls out of the machines, for each person and each agent. The portal's is made from it.
  • Clients follow things, not calls. The SDK hands back the thing. The hook has its state, what you can do, and what it needs.
  • Speed is judged per operation, as now. Waits are judged by their deadlines.
  • The next step goes. So does asking on an operation. Work that belongs to no single thing still has its order declared.

Where I would not go

Not every thing should be stored as an actor. Behave like one, yes. Be stored as one, only when it suits. A thing stored as an actor has no list and no rule across records, so the books would turn into views rebuilt from events. A thing that mostly waits, like a proposal, is a good actor. The books belong in a database.

What I am not sure of

  • A role written as code can't be listed, so its holders can be offered a move but not asked.
  • Telling people is a new part: an inbox, and how it reaches them.
  • "Once accepted, it finishes" for a database thing. I know what it should promise, not yet how it is built.
  • You model more things. Every intent that waits becomes one.

So it is not seal or ask. It is both, in two layers.

The model: things last, operations are short, and a waiting thing says what it needs. That is your idea, and it replaces solutions two and three.

The shortcut: when the other half is only data, seal it, and there is nothing to wait for.

In Capxul Lite that gives one of each. A spend is sealed: one act, one signature. A grant is a proposal that needs its owners.

Where the order belongs

You corrected me here, and you are right. I said a workflow's order lives in its handler, "the layer you should not have to read". That mixed up two questions: whether a person reads something, and whether it is declared.

  • The test for the blueprint is not "does a person read it". It is "does the framework need to know it", so it can check it, draw it, prove it, and say what a change touches. The order of anything that spans a wait passes that test. So it is declared, at a level you may never open. The agent writing the blueprint writes it.
  • The fault is in the shape, not the idea. As M3.01 decided it, a workflow declares its activities, and the order and the branches sit in a handler the framework can't see into. That is the part I would change. The engine can stay underneath.
  • Handlers keep what happens inside one commit. Order, branches, repeats and deadlines across a wait are data.

Work that belongs to no single thing

Paying forty people in one run. M3.01 chose code because a fixed list can't loop. A declared order can, if a thing can need all, any, or at least so many of a set of other things. A payroll run needs all of its payments. A grant needs at least two of its owners. It is the same "needs" as above, with a count.

Events, errors and the indexer

An event

Every move records one. It is written in the same commit as the change. On chain it is a Solidity event, with who signed and their nonce added in front. Events are the only thing that crosses from one place to another.

An error

Declared once, and named on the operation or the rule that can raise it. It gets one code, kept in a lock file that only ever grows. So "budget exceeded" is the same name and the same code from the contract, the API, the SDK, the command line, an agent's tool and the app.

The indexer

  • One per contract, per environment. It is an actor.
  • It knows the events from the model. Nobody hands it an interface file. It turns raw logs back into the events you declared.
  • It keeps its place, waits as many blocks as the environment says, and hands on only what the chain will keep. If a confirmed event is undone later, it hands on a correction.
  • It feeds four things: operations started by an event, anything waiting on one, the list of budgets, because a contract has no list, and the portal and traces.

The factory, sketched

  • The framework generates the factory. Opening a treasury deploys that company's contract.
  • An environment binds the factory to a network. There is one factory per network.
  • The indexer follows the factory, and every contract it has made.
  • A treasury's id and its address are the same fact, said two ways.

Changing a live contract, sketched

  • You change a rule. Generate makes a new version of the contract. Bringing the environment up publishes it. Nothing live changes.
  • New companies get the new version.
  • Upgradeable: a company's owners, together, take the new version in place.
  • Not upgradeable: the owners, together, move. A new contract is opened, the money and the budgets move across, and the old one closes.
  • The compiler compares how each version stores its data. If an upgrade would corrupt it, the change has to be a move.
  • The portal shows which company is on which version.

That turns the largest hole into something the "change" sheet can say honestly: the model tells you what is stale, and what it takes for money that is already out there.
