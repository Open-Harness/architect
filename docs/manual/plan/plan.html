Orthograph · the manual · a plan before it is rebuilt

One app, from an idea to a system you trust

The manual should read as one story. It opens with the problem and the answer, then follows a single app from the first conversation about it to a change made months later. This page is the plan for that, set in the type I am proposing.

What changes

Four changes to the pilot

  1. It opens with the problem and the solution. Today it opens with a page about roles, before you know why there are roles.
  2. It gains a first chapter: planning. Before anything is described, you and an agent talk the system through until it fits on one page. Nothing in the pilot covers this.
  3. It has one spine. Every chapter is a stage in the life of Plugged In, in order, grouped into four parts. The roles and the kit move to the back, as reference, and are met in the story as they appear.
  4. It reads like a book, not a dashboard. A serif for reading, a quiet sans for structure, the films' navy only for terminals, and contents that stay in view.

Chapter 0 · Why this exists

The hard part is the next morning

An agent can write a working system in an afternoon. The contract, the server, the database, the app, the command line, the tools for other agents. Writing it was never the hard part.

The hard part is the next morning. Is it right? Is it fast enough, and what happens when something fails? And when one rule changes, what else has to change? To answer any of that, someone has to read all of it. Usually that someone is you.

Orthograph takes a position. You work on the system: the things in it, who may do what, and what must always be true. That description is the blueprint, and it is the part you read. Everything below it, an agent writes and the framework makes. And nobody takes anyone's word for it: the running system is proved, against stories you agreed to.

Youdecide the system, and read the blueprint and the journeys
Your agentsplan with you, write the stories, build the handlers, review each other
The frameworkmakes the server, the API, the SDK, the tools and the deployment, and proves them

This manual follows one small app, Plugged In, from the first conversation about it to a change made months after it went live. Each chapter is one stage. It says what you do, what your agents do, what you read, and what goes wrong, and it has a film. Everything shown was recorded from a real run, and each chapter says plainly what does not exist yet.

Chapter 1

Plan it: the chapter that is missing

Before anything is described, you talk the system through with an agent until it fits on one page. The page is what you argue with. Code comes later.

What you do

  • Say the idea in one sentence.
  • Answer questions, one at a time. Say "I don't know" when you don't.
  • Argue with the page until it is right. Reject what is wrong, and say why.

What your agent does

  • Says your idea back, as strongly as it can, before judging it.
  • Draws one page: the things, who may do what, what must stay true, what waits on what, where two places have to agree.
  • When you reject something, starts again from the facts, with options graded on one rubric.
  • Ends with the doubts it could not settle.

What you read

  • One page. Nothing else. If you can't see the whole system on it, it isn't planned yet.
  • The list of doubts. Each becomes a spike, or a blank in the blueprint.

What goes wrong

  • The agent plans a feature, not a system. A small whole thing shows the joins; a feature hides them.
  • The page says how, not what. A plan names things and rules, never files and functions.
  • A doubt gets decided by guessing. It should stay a blank until someone answers it.

How it would stand. This is how this repository plans, every day, but it is practice and not a command. Nothing in Orthograph runs it, and there is nothing to record from ortho. To keep the manual's rule that nothing is invented, I would run a real planning session for Plugged In with an agent, keep the page it draws and the conversation as the chapter's recordings, and film that. The agent stays neutral: any agent that can draw a page will do.

The look

A book, with a terminal in it

  • Serif for reading. Source Serif at 19 pixels, lines about 70 characters long.
  • A quiet sans for structure. IBM Plex Sans for labels and contents, the same face as the films.
  • Terminals in the films' navy. The only dark blocks on the page, so recorded output stands out.
  • Contents always in view. On a wide screen they sit in a rail on the left.
  • Each chapter opens the same way. Its number, its question, how it stands, its film, then its four parts.
  • Fewer boxes and capitals. Lines and space do the work. Labels are small and few.

What happens to what exists

Nothing written is lost

  • The three pilot chapters keep their numbers, 4, 5 and 7, and their films. Their words are kept; only their openers change to the new pattern.
  • "Working with your agents" moves to the back. Its five-line cast goes into the opening, and each role is introduced in the chapter where it first appears: the designer in chapter 1, the spike in 2, the author in 4, the builder in 5, the reviewer in 6.
  • The chapters not yet written appear in the contents, in place, marked "to write", so the whole journey is visible from the start.
  • The check and the recorder stay. Every quote is still checked against a real recording.
  • One page, while it holds about a dozen chapters. If it grows past that, a page per chapter with the same contents rail.
