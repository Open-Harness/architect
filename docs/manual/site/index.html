For the person who directs the work and reads the blueprint. One small app, Plugged In, from the first conversation about it to a change made months later, in eleven chapters, each with a film.

The hard part is the next morning

An agent can write a working system in an afternoon. The server, the database, the command line, the tools for other agents, the page of docs. Writing it was never the hard part.

The hard part is the next morning. Is it right? Is it fast enough, and what happens when something fails? When one rule changes, what else has to change? To answer any of that, someone has to read all of it. Usually that someone is you.

What Orthograph does about it

We want you to be able to trust a system you did not write and have not read. So Orthograph moves the part you read.

You work on the system itself: the things in it, who may do what to them, and what must always be true. That description is the blueprint, and it is small enough to read whole. Everything below it is either made by the framework from the blueprint, or written by an agent inside a frame the blueprint sets. And nobody takes anyone's word that it works. The running system is proved against stories you agreed to before it was built.

So three parties do three different jobs. You decide the system, and you read the blueprint and the stories. Your agents plan with you, write the stories, build the behaviour and review each other. The framework makes the server, the API, the SDK, the command line and the deployment, and proves them.

How to read this manual

The manual follows one small app, Plugged In, from the first conversation about it to a change made months after it went live. A person claims a name, and gets a public page of links. It is small on purpose, so you can hold all of it in your head. It has no money and nothing that waits on anyone else yet; its next stage adds both, and the first chapter plans it.

Each chapter is one stage in that life, in the order it happens. It says what you do, what your agents do and what you read, and it ends with what goes wrong and with how that part of Orthograph stands today. Each chapter has a film of two or three minutes that shows the same thing happening.

Everything printed in a chapter was recorded from a real run, or is quoted from a file, and a check fails the manual if it is not. Where something does not exist yet, the chapter says so in the same voice as everything else.

The cast

Five roles appear in the story, and one agent takes one role at a time. The designer turns what you want into a plan and then a blueprint. The author writes the stories that say what done means. The builder writes one handler until those stories pass. The reviewer reads a change it had no part in. And a spike answers one doubtful question in throwaway code. Each is introduced where it first appears, and all five are set out in full at the back.

When it goes wrong

The commonest way to go wrong with this manual is to skip to the building. The chapters before it are short, and they are where the decisions are made that the later chapters only carry out.

How this stands

Orthograph is not finished, and the manual says where. All eleven chapters are written, and each says in its last section what runs today, what is only our practice, and what is missing. The commands in them were recorded on Plugged In. The way of working with agents that runs through all of them is how this repository builds Orthograph itself, every day, and it is practice: no command hands a new app its roles yet, and nothing stops an agent stepping outside one. Two chapters stop short on purpose. Planning stops at the first question its page asks the owner, and staging stops at the plan, because the real deployment has not been recorded yet.

Part one

Before any code

01Part one · Before any code

Plan it

What is the system, and what don't we know yet?

Before anything is described to the framework, you talk the system through with an agent until it fits on one page. The page is what you argue with. Code comes later, and so does the blueprint.

Say the idea in one sentence

We want the plan to start from what you mean, in your words, and not from a feature list. Plugged In already lets a person claim a name and keep a page of links. Its next stage began as one sentence from its owner: people should be able to pay each other by name, a handle gets a wallet, and anyone can send money to that name.

That is all the designer is given. It reads the app as it stands, and it reads nothing about how the idea might be built.

The agent says it back, as one page

The first thing a designer does is say your idea back, as strongly as it can, and say what it is not.

Then it draws the system, and only the system: the things and the states each can be in, who may do what, and what must stay true. It names no file and no function. Here is what it said must stay true.

Read those as the owner. The second one is a rule you might never have said aloud, and it decides what happens when a name changes hands with a payment on its way. That is what the page is for.

What waits, and where two places must agree

Two parts of the page are where real systems go wrong, and a good plan makes them plain. The first is anything that takes time or another party. On the page, something that waits is a thing in a state, and it says what it is waiting for.

The second is every join between two places that hold the same fact.

It ends with doubts, not answers

A plan is finished when it says what it does not know. Each doubt is one question, and each is sorted: something to try in throwaway code, which is the next chapter, or a decision only you can make, which stays a blank until you make it.

And it asks you one question first, with its reason.

What you read

One page, and nothing else. If you cannot see the whole system on it, it is not planned yet. You answer one question at a time, and you say "I don't know" when you don't. When something on the page is wrong, you reject it and say why, and the designer starts again from the facts.

When it goes wrong

The agent plans a feature and not a system. A small whole thing shows its joins, and a feature hides them. If the page has no part for what waits and no part for where two places must agree, send it back.

The page says how, and not what. A plan names things and rules. The moment it names a file, a table or a function, it has stopped being something you can argue with.

A doubt gets settled by guessing. On this page the agent marked its guesses, each with the word. A guess that loses its mark becomes a decision nobody made.

How this stands

This is how we plan in this repository, and it is practice: no command runs a planning session, and nothing in Orthograph draws the page. The page above is real. An agent drew it on 26 November 2026 from the one sentence and the app as it stands, and it is printed as it was written. It is the first half of a session only: the owner has not yet answered its first question, so nothing on it is decided, and the film shows that first half and stops there.

02Part one · Before any code

Spike what you doubt

Will the part I'm unsure of actually work?

The plan for "pay by name" fits on one page, and the page lists seven doubts. Five are decisions only you can make. Two are questions nobody can answer by thinking harder. If you describe the system now, you build it on a guess, and you find out which guess was wrong after the journeys are written.

Give one agent one question

We want an answer before the design depends on it, and we want it cheaply. So one agent takes the spike role and is given one line of the page, as it is written there.

That sentence is the whole task. The agent may write throwaway code in a folder of its own under spikes/, and it may touch nothing in the app. It builds the smallest thing that answers the question, and then it tries to break it.

Play every order, and count what breaks

A late reversal is a question about order: the money came in, some of it left, and then the provider took the payment back. So the spike builds no wallet and calls no provider. It keeps a small ledger for one wallet and plays the same eight events in every order they can happen.

Two thirds of the orders are safe, because a payment that is still held can simply be returned. The doubt lives in the other 840, and inside those, in the 225 where the holder has already taken money out.

Each way of mirroring breaks a different rule

The spike then tries three things the app could do with a late reversal, and holds each to rules taken from the plan page. The plain one is to take the money out of the balance.

Read the example as the owner. Nothing in it is unusual: a payment, a withdrawal, a reversal. Refusing to mirror the reversal keeps the balance above zero, and makes it untrue.

The third way takes what is in the wallet and writes the rest down beside it.

The balance holds, and two rules break as they are written: the one quoted, and the agreement with the provider, in the same 225 orders. Both hold again once the shortfall is counted in them. And in 150 orders somebody is still owed money when the events run out.

One page is kept

The spike ends by writing one page, and the page opens with the question and the answer.

Below that it says what was tried, what was found, with the counts, and what was not tried. It decides nothing. It says what you now have to decide, before the blueprint is written.

The code is thrown away. It stays in its folder as the evidence for the page, and the app never imports it.

What you read

The page, and nothing else. You read the answer, then the open risks, and you take both back to the plan. Here the plan changes in one of two ways, and which one depends on the question the designer asked you first: who holds the money.

When it goes wrong

The spike answers a different question. An agent that finds a more interesting one will chase it. The page must open with the sentence it was given, and a better question goes into the page as a note for you.

The spike only shows that the idea works. One order of events, mirrored at once, with the reversal arriving early, would have printed nothing broken. Ask what it did to break the idea, and send it back if the answer is nothing.

The code gets kept. It works, so someone copies the ledger into the app. It was written to answer a question and was never proved against a journey, and it should be written again from the blueprint.

The answer gets read as a decision. This page says a shortfall would keep the balance at zero or above. It does not say the app should have one. That is still yours to say.

And a spike can be too small to trust. This one played one wallet and one reversal, with no provider. Its page says so, under open risks, and the plan should carry those risks forward and not the comfort.

How this stands

Spiking is this repository's practice, and no command runs one: nothing in Orthograph starts a spike, names its folder or checks its page. The spike above is real and small. An agent ran it on 26 November 2026 for this chapter, and its run and its page are printed as recorded. It was run on a design nobody has agreed, because the plan page's first question is still unanswered, so it supports no decision yet.

The page's other spike, whether a provider can open a wallet for each person, was not run. And nothing in Orthograph stops an app importing spike code. That it never does is a rule we keep by hand.

03Part one · Before any code

Describe it

How does the plan become something the framework can check?

The plan page is agreed as far as it goes. It names a wallet and its states, and it still has seven doubts on it, five of them marked blank. Now the page has to become something the framework can check, and the parts you have not decided must not be decided for you on the way.

Declare what the page names

We want the framework to hold the plan's nouns, and nothing more than the plan says. The designer does not write the blueprint from an empty file. It asks for each thing and each operation by name, and the framework writes the frame.

The first command wrote seven files and the second wrote one. None of them holds a decision. Where a decision goes, the frame has a blank, and the blank says in words what is missing.

Every blank is a question with an address

We want to see what is undecided as a list, not find it by reading. The check prints each blank with its file and its line, and counts them.

Seven questions, and the designer reports them to you as questions. The model says the same thing more briefly. It lists the wallet beside the things that are finished, with no states yet, and then says what is not done.

Fill only what the page already says

Two of the seven are answered on the plan page, where it names the wallet: a handle's money, one per claimed handle, with four states. So the designer fills those two, in the words of the page.

The first state is where a wallet starts. The moves between them are still empty, and that is honest: the page says what a wallet in opening waits for, and nobody has yet said who holds the money. The check now counts five.

The designer stops there. Who may open a wallet depends on the page's first question, and you have not answered it.

The generated files have fallen behind

The same check has a second complaint, further down. The blueprint changed, so the files made from it are stale.

Generating does not wait for the blanks. It writes what it can for the wallet as it stands.

So the unfinished thing is generated: it gets its types, its page of docs and its table. The unfinished operation is left out, and no handler is written for it. After this the check has one complaint left.

What you read

You read the blueprint files the designer changed, which here is two lines, and the list of blanks. Each blank is either a question you can answer now or one of the doubts from the plan. The count is the thing to watch. It should go down only when you decide something.

When it goes wrong

Generating too early writes a file that does not compile. We recorded it: with the wallet's states still blank, ortho generate wrote the wallet's types anyway, and the check then failed on them.

Fill a thing's states before you generate.

A designer will sometimes fill a blank the page does not answer. The check cannot tell a decision from a guess: a blank that is gone is simply gone. So when the count drops, ask which line of the plan each answer came from.

The check fails for as long as one blank is left, and it says check ✘ whether the blueprint is broken or only unfinished. Read the parts above that line. Nothing yet tells the two apart for you.

Not everything undecided becomes a blank. The frame left the wallet's moves empty and wrote its rules as an empty file, and the check counts neither. What must stay true on the plan page is not in the blueprint until the designer writes it.

How this stands

Adding a thing and an operation, the check's list of blanks, the model's unfinished lines and generating all run today, and everything above was recorded on Plugged In on 26 November 2026. The two blanks were filled by the recording itself, in the words of the plan page; no agent was filmed designing, and the wallet was removed from the example afterwards. That a designer leaves a blank for what you have not decided, and reports the blanks to you as questions, is this repository's practice and not a command. Orthograph has one kind of blank today. A second kind, a question that carries its options for a decision not made yet, is designed and not built, and so is the ready check meant to go with it, a way to ask only whether any blank remains.

04Part two · Build it

Say what done means

How will I know it's right without reading the code?

An agent can write a handler in minutes. So the question is never whether it can build this. The question is how you will know it is right. The answer is to say what done means before anyone builds, and to have someone other than the builder say it.

Done is a story

In Orthograph, done is a journey: a short story with a cast, told through real calls to the real system. Each step is a sentence you can say yes or no to, and under the sentence is a call and what must be true once it returns.

The first line under the name is the call. After it come the answer, the event, the state and the spans of the trace. You do not check the code against this. You read the sentence, and decide whether it is what you meant.

Stubs are made while planning

We want a story to exist for every path before anyone thinks about building, so adding them belongs to planning. The designer does it, with one command for each thing in the blueprint. The command keeps any journey already written.

Nine files: one for each path through the handle's states, one for each role, one for each error an operation declares, and one for each refusal a rule gives, such as a name that is already taken. A stub already knows who may call and who must be turned away. What it cannot know, it leaves blank, and the check lists every blank as a question, with its file and its line. A blank is a question for you, never a default.

An author fills them in

A second agent, the author, fills the blanks and writes the stories the stubs could not guess. It is a different agent from the one that will build, and that is the point: a builder who also writes the stories writes down what its code does, not what you meant. A builder adds nothing here, and never edits a journey.

The first run is red on purpose

Then the author has to show something odd: that its journeys can fail. It runs every one of them on handlers that do nothing.

Seven journeys, fifty-six steps, fifty of them red. That is the result you want, because a check that cannot fail proves nothing. And every red line says what it expected.

Six steps are green already, and they are marked.

The blueprint already says who may call, so the runtime turns a stranger away before any handler runs. Nobody has to build those.

What you read

Before a single handler exists, you hold two things. You hold the stories, which you read as sentences from top to bottom and agreed. And you hold a red run, which shows they bite. This is the cheapest moment in the whole life of the system to change your mind.

When it goes wrong

A journey that is green on the empty run, and is not marked as proved by the description, cannot fail. It proves nothing, and it goes back to the author.

A stub is sometimes left as it was written. The check keeps listing its blanks, and ortho model shows the file as unfinished, until someone answers them.

Stories tend to cover the path where everything works. Count the refusals: every one the blueprint declares needs a step that causes it. The full run counts them for you, on its coverage line, in the next chapter.

The journey commands are rough in three places today, and they are being fixed. The check lists blanks only inside the blueprint's folder; Plugged In keeps its finished journeys in a folder of their own beside it, and both are read. Adding stubs turns the check red, which is right while they are blanks, and it also reports drift in what was generated until that is made again. And adding one journey by name writes a stub with a blank cast, and every command that reads the model then stops with an error, where it should ask a question, until the cast is filled in.

How this stands

Every command here runs today, and everything printed was recorded on Plugged In on 26 November 2026. That a different agent writes the stories is how we work in this repository. No command starts an agent in a role, and nothing in a new app stops a builder editing a journey: you keep the split by how you hand out the work.

05Part two · Build it

Build until green

How do I hand work to an agent and get back something proved?

The journeys are agreed, and every one of them is red. Now an agent has to write the code that turns them green, and you have to believe the result without reading that code.

Hand over one brief

We want the builder to know exactly two things: what it may write, and when it is done. You don't write that task yourself. You ask for it, by the name of one operation.

The brief also says what to read and what the handler must do, left out here. One line of it tells the builder never to refuse a taken name itself, because the runtime does that from a rule. Hand it over as it is printed. Anything you add in your own words is something the builder can misread.

Start from the scaffold

The builder never starts from an empty file. The framework has already written the frame of the handler, with a blank where the behaviour goes. So the first thing the builder does is run the one journey, by the name it declares or by its file, and read what fails.

Four of eight steps pass before a line is written. Those are the steps that never needed this handler. One failure names the blank the handler reached, and the next names the file it is in. That is where the builder goes.

Same count, a different failure

The builder writes the work: two records, saved together, the handle and its public page. Then it runs the journey again.

The number has not moved, and the builder has. Before, the server died. Now it answered without making its move: the records were saved and the state never changed. Not every rung turns a light green. Every rung changes what fails first.

Green on one journey is not done

One line makes the move, and the journey passes, and so does the run.

Read the line before the verdict. One journey does not reach every operation, so this run does not judge coverage: it says so, and points at the whole run. The builder finishes by running all of it, and then the check.

The full run judges nine evals for each operation, and a journey can pass while one of them fails. The coverage line counts the moves, the outcomes and the refusals that some journey reached.

What you read

You read none of the handler. You read one line for each journey, and the last line of the full run. When the count is stuck, you read the reasons, because that is where the movement shows. And you ask for the whole run before you believe a report: a builder that says "it passes" from one journey has told you less than it thinks.

When it goes wrong

A builder will sometimes say a journey is wrong. Then it stops, and tells the author. It does not edit the story and it does not work round it. You decide who is right by reading the story, which is the one thing here written for you.

A step will sometimes fail in a handler the builder was not given. At the first rung the journey also failed in two other handlers, because nothing had been claimed yet. The builder fixes its own handler and runs again. It does not touch the others.

A handler will sometimes grow a rule: it starts checking something the blueprint never said. That is a finding for the reviewer, and the fix is to move the rule into the blueprint, where you can read it.

And sometimes everything is green and the app is still wrong. Then the journeys do not say what you meant, and the work goes back a chapter.

How this stands

Everything printed above was recorded on Plugged In. The climb is a replay: we put one handler at three points and recorded the real run at each, and no agent was filmed writing it. That a builder may touch one handler and nothing else is how we work in this repository. Nothing in Orthograph enforces it for you yet.

06Part two · Build it

Review without reading the code

Is this change what I asked for, and nothing more?

A change has come back. You asked for two small things about claiming a name: say what it does more plainly, and hold it to a hundred milliseconds where it had a hundred and fifty. Nine files changed. Is this what you asked for, and nothing more? You should not have to read nine files to find out.

Give it to someone who wrote none of it

We want the change judged by an agent with nothing to defend. So the reviewer is a fresh agent that had no part in the work, and it is given two things: the change, and the brief the change was made from. Here the brief is your two sentences. Had the change been a handler, the brief would be what ortho brief printed in the last chapter, and one line of it would be the reviewer's first test.

The reviewer touches nothing. It reads, it runs commands, and it reports.

Start with the part you can read

The reviewer opens the blueprint's part of the change first, because that is the part you will read.

Two lines out and two lines in, in one operation's declaration. Those are the two things you asked for. Then it lists every file the change touched.

One file is the blueprint's and the other eight are under generated/. No handler is in the list, and no journey. Nobody reads the eight. A check, further down, says whether they are exactly what the blueprint makes.

Ask what the change unproved

We want to know how far the change reaches, and one command says. It needs a machine that ran the whole proof before the change arrived, because it compares with the record that proof left. Before the change it had nothing to report.

With the change in place, it says this.

The first line names the declaration that changed, and what in it. The second names the one operation whose proof no longer holds, and it is stale until it is proved again. The other six stand.

Run the proof yourself

A report that says "it passes" is a claim. So the reviewer runs the whole proof, and then the check.

Every journey passes, and claim is now measured against the new budget.

The drift line is what lets everyone skip the eight generated files: each is exactly what the blueprint generates. Asked once more, ortho impact answers stale none, as it did before the change.

What you read

You read four lines of blueprint, and the reviewer's findings. A finding names a file and a line, what is wrong, and what would make it right. The reviewer fixes nothing itself. What the commands show here is a change that did what was asked: two declared lines, one operation unproved and proved again, and nothing outside the blueprint and what it generates.

When it goes wrong

A change is handed over before ortho generate was run. Then the check fails, and names every file that is behind.

The reviewer reports it and sends the change back. It does not run the command to tidy up.

An operation goes stale and no line says what changed. After the proof above, we added one comment line to claim's handler and left the blueprint alone.

The blueprint did not move, and the code did. That is the one time the reviewer reads a handler, looking for a decision made in code that belongs in the blueprint.

The record is missing, or already overwritten. On a fresh checkout ortho impact has nothing to compare with, and lists every operation as unproven. And a full proof writes a new record, so a reviewer that proves first and asks second is told nothing is stale. Ask first.

A budget is not a gate. The performance eval never fails: its own notes (packages/prove/src/modules/evals/README.md) say a p95 over one and a half times the budget adds a review line and still passes. We did not record one. The reviewer reads the numbers on that line, not only the mark.

How this stands

Every command above runs today and was recorded on Plugged In on 26 November 2026. It was staged. The recording made the change itself, two edits and one ortho generate, and added the comment line to the handler; no agent wrote the change and no reviewer was filmed reading it. The example was put back afterwards.

That the reviewer wrote none of the change, and that a builder never edits the journeys that judge it, is how we work in this repository. No command checks who wrote what; the reviewer reads the list of files. ortho brief prints a brief for one handler only, so a change to the blueprint has no printed brief to be held against. The record ortho impact reads sits in a folder git ignores, so it does not travel with a change or reach CI. And after this change, the comment at the top of claim's handler still quotes the old description and the old budget, and nothing says so.

Part three

Run it

07Part three · Run it

Run it, break it, read the trace

When something looks wrong, how do I find out why?

Everything is green. So you try the app yourself, and something looks wrong. You want to know why, and you do not want to read the code to find out.

Run it, and say what you saw

One command runs the app on your machine, with a local world and no cloud account.

Plugged In has no screens yet, so we call it through the command line tool that was generated from its blueprint. We claim a name, and then a second one.

The tool printed the refusal. The last line is the status it ended with, written down by the recording. Is that a bug? You don't have to know. "I asked for a second name and it said no" is enough to hand to your agent.

Find the one call

We want the agent to read what happened, not to guess at it. Every answer the app gives carries the id of its trace, so the agent makes the same call again and keeps that id.

Then it prints that one trace.

Read it

A trace is everything one call did. The operation's line says which operation ran, how long it took, and that it was refused, with the refusal's name. The line under it is the cause: a rule, by its name in the blueprint.

What is missing matters as much. The record step ran, and there is no commit: the rule was checked after the work and before anything was saved. A call that was answered ends differently.

The line that gave it

So the refusal did not come from the handler at all. It came from six lines of blueprint.

Now the question is yours, and it is a real one. Should a person hold only one name? If yes, the app is right and the message needs work. If no, the rule changes, and that is a later chapter.

When it goes wrong

A refusal and a defect are different things, and a trace marks them differently. Refused means a declared answer, with a code. A defect is the server failing, and it goes to a builder with the trace attached. We did not record a defect for this chapter: it is marked with a ✘, as the telemetry command's own notes say (packages/cli/src/modules/telemetry/README.md).

An agent will sometimes explain a refusal from the handler. Here the handler never refuses a second name: the runtime does, from the rule. If an explanation does not name a line of the blueprint, ask for the trace.

And sometimes the trace is not there yet. The Collector writes its files a moment after the call, so wait a few seconds. A trace recorded before the app was stopped still prints afterwards, because the command reads files.

How this stands

Running the app, calling it and printing a trace all run today, and everything above was recorded on Plugged In. Two parts are missing. No command signs a test person in to a local app, so the recording asked the local world's own door for a session, as journeys do. And the generated command line tool prints a refusal's code and not its trace's id, which is why the agent repeats the call to read the id from the answer.

08Part three · Run it

Stage it

How does it get from my machine to a real address?

Plugged In is green on your machine, and you have run it there and read a trace. Nobody else can reach it. To give it a real address, something has to be made in a cloud account that you pay for, and what runs there has to be the thing that was proved, not something built again on the way. You want to see both before anything is made.

Read where it will run

We want to know what staging is before we ask for it. It is one short file of the blueprint, an environment, and you can read it whole.

One server on Cloudflare, one database, and real sign-in where your machine had fixed test people. Staging also needs a value for every secret the app declares, so we ask what it declares.

Plugged In declares none, so nothing is owed. An app that had some would see their names here, never their values.

Ask for the plan before anything is made

We want the list of what will exist in the account, and we want it without touching the account. A staging in the cloud is made for one run, and you give the run a short name. Given the word plan, the command that makes it prints what it would make, and stops.

Read it as the owner. Three things will exist: the server, its database, and a namespace that belongs to the server. Every name starts with the prefix, and the prefix is the app, the environment and the run. That is also how the run is cleaned up. We recorded ortho env destroy staging --plan --run demo1 too, and it prints this same document.

Build it once

We want staging to run the exact file that was proved, so the app is built once, from a commit, and never again for another environment.

The first word after "built" is the commit, cut short. The long one is the fingerprint of the file. Those two are what you will ask any address for.

Run the same file here first

Before the account comes into it, the same file can run as staging on your machine. With a port and no run, ortho env up staging starts it and leaves it running. Then we ask it what it is.

The same commit and the same fingerprint that the build printed. It stays up until you stop it.

The step that needs your account

Now the real one: the same command, without the word plan.

This is as far as we recorded. The command wants two values from your cloud account, and without them it stops before it contacts anything. They are yours. You put them in your own shell, or in the secret store of wherever the command runs, and no agent is handed them or prints them.

What follows is what the command's own notes say (packages/cli/src/modules/staging/README.md), and none of it is recorded here. With the two values, it makes the database, uploads the built file, turns the address on, and waits for three good answers before it gives you the address. When it goes wrong

The build refuses when anything in the repository is not committed, because a bundle has to be one commit.

A run's name is small on purpose, because it becomes part of every name in the account.

Proving a staging has to know where your account puts its Workers, and it will not guess.

Moving a proved build on by its verdicts did not get as far as a verdict on Plugged In.

We tried that as well, and it stops too.

Those four describe a pipeline, not a person at a desk. So on your own machine you stage with the commands above, and promote waits for a pipeline.

And a deploy can die halfway. The notes say destroy needs nothing from the run that failed: it lists the account and deletes what carries the run's prefix. We have not recorded that either.

How this stands

The plan, the build, staging on this machine and every refusal above run today, and were recorded on Plugged In on 26 November 2026 with no cloud account. The real deployment in this chapter is not recorded. It waits on a cloud credential from this repository's owner, and it will be recorded when there is one. Watch it

Is it healthy, and what changed this week?

Plugged In is running somewhere, and people are calling it. Nobody is running journeys against those calls. So the question changes: is it healthy, and what changed this week? You want that answer on one screen, and you still do not want to read the code.

The budget is already written down

We want "fast enough" to be a number you agreed to, not a feeling. It already is. Every operation in the blueprint carries a budget, and the page a visitor reads has one too.

The proof has been holding the app to that number since chapter 5. A full run prints one such line for each operation.

Those are the journeys' own calls. They say the app can be fast enough. They say nothing about this week.

Watch judges what an environment recorded

One command reads the calls an environment recorded and judges them. Before the environment has run, it has nothing to read, and it says so.

So we start the test environment on this machine, which stays up until we stop it and keeps what it records.

Then we use it, as in chapter 7. We claim a name, ask for a second and are refused, and read the public page three times. Now there is something to watch.

Read it from the top. Each operation that was called has its own line, measured against its own budget. Then the calls are counted, and the refusals by name and code. One refusal is not a problem, because a refusal is the app saying no on purpose. Defects are the app breaking, and there are none. The evals line is the proof again, on real calls. Speed is the lines above it. Four more evals can be judged from what a call leaves behind, and the line ends by naming the four that only the proof can judge. The window is the last hour unless you ask for another with --since.

The same calls, over a budget

Nothing on our machine was slow. To see what slow looks like, we tightened one budget in the blueprint, from 150 milliseconds to 10, read the page twenty-five more times, and watched again.

The operation is marked, and named again on the drift line. And watch wrote a file: a task, ready to hand to a builder.

Everything the earlier chapters taught is on that page. The slowest calls are named by their trace, so the builder reads one before it guesses.

In that trace the load step ran three times, and one of them took most of the time. The work is asked for with a brief, as in chapter 5. And the last line is for you: making the handler faster is a builder's job, and loosening the budget is your decision. We put the budget back, and the same twenty-eight calls were healthy again.

What you read

One screen. You read the mark beside each operation and the drift line. Then you read the counts, because they are where a week shows: an operation nobody calls, or a refusal that was rare and is now most of the traffic. Watch does not judge a count. It prints it, and you decide what it means.

When it goes wrong

Calls made to the app under ortho dev are not watched. We made three before the recording above, and watch counted none of them. It read the proof's own folder and left the proof's calls out, as it should.

On a checkout where the proof had never run, the same command could not run at all when we tried it by hand, and it told us to start an environment called local. That advice is wrong.

An environment runs a revision that was committed. With one stray file in the folder, ours would not start.

A few calls are not a pattern. With the tight budget and only three reads, watch said the operation was over and would not call it drift. It needs twenty calls. The operation's own line still carried a tick, so read the thin line.

And watch judges against the blueprint in your folder, not the one the environment is running. That is how we moved a budget without restarting anything. It also means an uncommitted edit changes the verdict.

How this stands

Everything above was recorded on 26 November 2026 on Plugged In, in the test environment on one machine. That is not a real address, and the traffic was ours: two claims and twenty-eight reads. The drift was staged. Nothing was slow, so we tightened a budget in the blueprint for the length of the recording and put it back. We read in watch's own notes (packages/cli/src/modules/watch/README.md) that an eval failing on real calls fails the watch, and that --source axiom reads a hosted store. We recorded neither: no eval failed on our calls, and Axiom was not available to us.

Two things are missing. Watch prints one window and compares it with nothing, so "what changed this week" is you reading this week's screen beside last week's. And nothing runs it for you: no schedule, and no message when an operation drifts. Someone has to ask.

More than one agent

How do several agents work at once without stepping on each other?

So far one agent has worked on Plugged In at a time: a designer, then an author, then a builder with one brief. Pay by name is bigger than that. You will want several agents building at once, and two agents in the same files undo each other's work without either of them noticing.

Orthograph has no command for this yet. So this chapter leaves Plugged In, and shows how this repository builds Orthograph itself with many agents at once. Three things do the work: a claim, one rule for collisions, and a list that may only shrink.

Every line of work has one owner

We want each line of work to belong to one agent you can name. Here that owner is called a goal: one long-running agent that manages other agents, with a brief of its own. We asked the file that lists the goals which of them run.

Three run. One builds the framework and one builds an app on it. The third builds nothing new: it brings the app's finished work into the shared branch.

Claim before you build

We want an agent to learn that work is taken before it starts, and not when two changes meet. So a goal writes its claim down first, on the status page, which keeps one row for each piece of work. A claim is two cells of that row: the lane, which names the goal, and the paths the goal will change.

Find goal-16, and the two folders after it. Goal 16 holds this piece and has said where it will work. Any other agent that needs those folders reads this first. We counted the claims held today.

Thirteen rows are held by the app's goal and one by the framework's. On every other row the lane says none, and that work is free to take.

One rule when two agents want the same thing

An agent building an app keeps finding gaps in the framework under it. We want it to know at once whether a gap is its own to fix, without asking you. The same file answers with three cases.

Only the second case needs a decision, and the rule names the one agent that makes it. In the other two an agent reads the board and knows what to do: wait, or claim it and build.

A list that may only shrink

Journeys and checks are written before the build, by a different agent. With many agents that means red checks sit in the shared branch before the code that passes them. We want the shared branch to stay a gate anyway. So each failure we know about is written in a list, and the build fails on any failure that is not listed. Here is one whole list, about Plugged In's own journeys.

One line says why the entry is there and when it comes off. The other names the one check that may fail. A builder may delete a line from such a list and may never add one: a script in this repository's CI refuses a builder's pull request that does. So the lists are what is promised and not yet built, and you can count them.

Ten lists. The smallest holds one file, the one quoted above. You read these numbers the way you read red steps in a climb: over time they go down.

When it goes wrong

Two agents change the same file anyway. A claim is a cell in a table, and nothing locks the paths it names. Here one goal reads the claims every round, looking for two goals on one path, and settles each overlap as a decision. What it misses arrives as a merge conflict, a cost this repository accepts for running more at once.

A claim goes stale. The rule is that a goal which stops sets its lanes back to none. That relies on the goal doing it, so a row can stay held by an agent that is no longer running.

A builder wants a check changed. It stops and asks the check's author. An author's branch may add to a list, with its reason; a builder's may only take away.

And the tools around this are rough. The script that prints each list's numbers failed where we recorded, because it needs the network, so we counted files in plain shell. The dry run that prints overlapping claims needs GitHub and did not run there either, so we have no recording of an overlap being found.

How this stands

All of this is this repository's practice, and none of it is a command. The three recordings were made on 26 November 2026 by reading the repository's files; nothing was run on Plugged In. No command of Orthograph's claims work, reads a board, settles a collision or keeps a list of known failures. The list and the guard on a builder's pull request are scripts in this repository's own CI, and a new app gets neither.

The product's answer is designed and not built. It is called the factory, a board of work that agents build from, and the status page marks it as spiked. Until it exists, what you can copy is small: one file of claims, one rule, and one list that only shrinks.

Reference

Working with your agents

You decide the system. Agents build it. This page is how that works without you reading the code: who does what, what you hand each of them, and how you know the work is right.

How it stands. The commands on this page run today. The roles are how this repository builds Orthograph itself, every day. Orthograph does not yet hand them to a new app: you copy the agent kit in.

The idea

An agent can write a handler in minutes. What it cannot give you is a reason to trust it. So trust is never asked for. It is arranged, by three things:

  1. You read the description, not the code. What the system is lives in the blueprint. Nothing important may live only in a handler.
  2. Done is said before the work starts, by someone else. A journey is a story through real operations. One agent writes the journeys. A different agent builds until they pass. The builder cannot change them.
  3. Every role has one thing it may touch. An agent that may only write one handler cannot quietly change what the system is.

The brief

ortho brief <thing>.<verb> prints everything a builder needs for one handler, and nothing else: the one file to write, the files to read, what the handler must do, what the runtime already does for it, the paths it must not touch, and what must pass. Hand it over as it is. A builder given only its brief has no room to wander.

The climb

Building is climbing. The builder runs the journeys, reads the first failure, fixes the handler, and runs them again. ortho prove --only "<journey's name>" runs one journey, by the name it declares or by its file. ortho prove runs them all, and only that run means done: a run of one journey does not judge coverage, and says so on the line before its verdict. ortho prove --empty runs them on empty handlers, which is how an author shows a journey can fail. You watch one number go down: the steps that are still red.

When it goes wrong

  • A builder says a journey is wrong. It stops and tells the author. It does not edit the journey, and it does not work round it. You decide which of them is right, by reading the journey.
  • Everything is green and the app is still wrong. The journeys do not say what you meant. That is the author's work, and the fix starts with a new journey that fails.
  • A handler grew a rule. If behaviour depends on a decision you never saw, it belongs in the blueprint as a rule, a role or a state. A reviewer looks for exactly this.
  • An agent reports success you cannot see. Ask which journeys ran. "It passes" with no run is not a report.

What Orthograph does not do for you yet

  • It does not put the kit into a new app.
  • The journey commands are rough, and are being fixed (26 November 2026). ortho check lists a blank only under blueprint/, and ortho add journey <name> writes a stub that stops the commands that read the model. The add commands write journeys to blueprint/journeys/, and Plugged In keeps its own in journeys/; both are read.
  • It has no command that starts an agent in a role, or that stops a builder editing a journey. This repository enforces that for itself in its own CI; an app has to keep the rule by how it hands out work.
  • It has no board of work that many agents build from. That is designed, and not built.
